Plumbing Vent Pipes: What They Do and How They Fail

Every drain needs air behind it. When a vent clogs, drains gurgle, slow down and smell.

Call (952) 866-9120

The pipe that sticks out of your roof is not a chimney. It is a plumbing vent, and its job is to let air into the drain system so wastewater flows smoothly and sewer gas escapes above the roof. When the vent is blocked, you hear it in the drains before you see it anywhere else.

This guide explains how vents work, the symptoms of a blocked or missing vent and what a plumber checks. It also covers a winter problem that Minnesota homeowners should know about.

What a vent does

Water running down a drain pulls air behind it. Without a path for that air, the water creates suction and pulls the water out of nearby traps, which breaks the seal that blocks sewer gas. A vent supplies air so the traps stay full and the drain runs quietly.

Most vents connect to the drain near each fixture and run up inside the wall to a stack through the roof. Some fixtures use an air admittance valve, a one-way valve that lets air in but not out, where code allows it.

Signs of a vent problem

SymptomWhat it suggests
Gurgling after a fixture drainsAir is pulling through the trap, often from a blocked vent
Slow drains in several fixturesA partial blockage in the vent or the drain line
Sewer smell inside the houseA dry trap or a failed vent or air admittance valve
Toilet bowl bubbles when another fixture drainsAir is trying to find a path through the toilet trap
Water level in a trap rises and fallsSiphoning from a poorly vented line

A slow drain with no gurgle is more likely a clog in the drain. If the symptoms come with a gurgle, a vent is a suspect. A plumber can often tell the difference by listening and by running water at several fixtures at once.

What blocks a vent

  • Leaves, nests and debris dropped into the roof opening.
  • A dead animal or a wasp nest in the stack.
  • Frost buildup at the top of the pipe in extreme cold, where warm moist air from the house freezes on the cold opening.
  • A crushed or disconnected section inside the wall.
  • A missing vent on an older or altered fixture.
Winter frost closure

In very cold weather, moisture in the warm air rising from the house can freeze at the top of the stack and narrow the opening. If drains gurgle in a deep freeze and then settle when it warms, ask a plumber to check the stack.

What a plumber checks

  1. Listen and test. The plumber runs water at several fixtures and listens for gurgling and observes how each drains.
  2. Check the roof stack. The top of the vent is inspected for debris, frost or a damaged cap.
  3. Run water or a line. A hose or a cable can show whether the stack is open down into the house.
  4. Inspect air admittance valves. A failed valve under a sink can leak air and smell.
  5. Plan the repair. The fix may be clearing a stack, replacing a valve or adding a vent where one is missing.

If the problem is a clog in the drain and not a vent, see drain cleaning. If you are adding a fixture or remodeling a bathroom, plumbing installation includes checking the vent layout.

Air admittance valves in plain terms

An air admittance valve is a spring-loaded one-way valve that opens when water drains and lets air in, then closes to keep sewer gas out. It can serve a fixture where running a vent through the roof is hard, such as an island sink. Local code decides where they are allowed, so a plumber should confirm before one is installed.

These valves wear out over time. A valve that fails can let air out, which brings a sewer smell, or fail to open, which brings gurgling and slow drains. Replacing one is a small job, and it is among the first things a plumber checks under a gurgling sink.

If you see a valve under your sink, do not cover it or block access to it, because it needs air around it to work.

If you are planning a bathroom or kitchen remodel, ask your plumber to check the vent layout before the walls are closed. Fixing a venting mistake after drywall is up costs far more than checking it during rough-in.

Local notes for Shakopee homes

The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry is the state licensing agency for plumbers, and its Check a License tool lets you confirm a license. Venting is part of the plumbing code, so changes to vents should be done by a licensed plumber and may need a permit.

The City of Shakopee building inspections page lists a Plumbing Permit and an ePortal. Ask the city whether a vent change needs one before starting work.

Shakopee homes range from older downtown houses to newer subdivisions. Older homes are more likely to have altered or missing vents, because fixtures were added over decades.

Source type: Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry and City of Shakopee building inspections page

Can I clear a vent myself?

Reaching the roof in winter is dangerous, and a fall is far worse than a gurgling sink. If you do check from the ground, look for a visible cap or a dark stack with frost on top and call a plumber for anything higher than a ladder rung. A plumber has the tools and the fall protection to do it safely.

Plumbing code note

Vent sizing, termination and air admittance valve use are set by the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted in Minnesota Rules chapter 4714.
