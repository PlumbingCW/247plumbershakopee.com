Running Toilet? How to Replace a Fill Valve at Home

A hissing or constantly running toilet is usually a worn fill valve or flapper. Most homeowners can fix it in under an hour.

Call (952) 866-9120

A toilet that hisses, refills on its own or runs for minutes after a flush is wasting water around the clock. In most cases the fill valve (the tall assembly on the left of the tank) or the flapper (the rubber seal at the bottom) has worn out. Both parts are cheap, and replacing a fill valve is a job most homeowners can finish in under an hour.

This guide helps you work out which part is failing, then walks through a fill valve swap step by step. If the toilet is cracked, leaking at the base or backing up, that is a different job and a plumber should look at it.

Is it the fill valve or the flapper?

What you noticeLikely cause
Hissing or trickling when the tank looks fullFill valve not shutting off, or water level set too high
Water running down the overflow tubeFill valve water level set too high, or a failing valve
Tank refills on its own every few minutesFlapper leaking water into the bowl
Handle must be held down to flushChain too short or long, or a worn flapper
Slow, noisy refillClogged valve or partly closed supply stop

A simple dye test separates the two. Drop a few drops of food coloring into the tank and wait about fifteen minutes without flushing. If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper is leaking. If the tank is full and the water keeps running, the fill valve is at fault.

Tools and parts

  • A new universal fill valve, sized to your tank height
  • An adjustable wrench or channel locks
  • A small bucket and a sponge or towel
  • A new braided supply line if the old one is stiff or corroded
  • A tape measure to match the valve height to your tank

Replace the fill valve in six steps

  1. Shut off the water. Turn the oval or round handle on the wall or floor stop clockwise until it stops. Flush once to empty the tank.
  2. Sponge out the rest. Use the sponge and bucket to remove the last water from the tank bottom.
  3. Disconnect the supply line. Unscrew the nut at the bottom of the tank under the valve. Have the bucket ready because a little water will spill.
  4. Remove the old valve. Unscrew the large nut under the tank that holds the valve in place, then lift the valve out from inside the tank.
  5. Install the new valve. Set the new valve in the hole, set its height as the instructions show so the top is above the overflow tube, and hand tighten the locknut. Do not use a wrench on plastic nuts.
  6. Reconnect, refill and adjust. Reattach the supply line, open the stop slowly, check for drips and set the water level to the mark in the tank.
The most common mistake

Overtightening plastic nuts cracks the tank. Hand tight plus a small extra turn is enough, and tightening a little more after the first fill is better than breaking the tank.

What a running toilet costs

A toilet that runs steadily can waste a lot of water over a day, and the bill does not care whether the water went to a flush or the drain. Homeowners in Shakopee should know that the utility bills for all water used, including water lost to leaks, so a quiet running toilet shows up on the bill before it shows up anywhere else.

If your bill jumped and you cannot find a leak, a running toilet is the first fixture to check. If the toilet is fine and the usage is still high, move on to a leak detection visit to check for a hidden line.

A quick check of the flapper too

While the tank is open, lift the flapper and feel its edge. A flapper that is soft, sticky or covered in grit will leak slowly even with a new fill valve. Clean the seat with a cloth and replace the flapper if it looks worn.

Local notes for Shakopee toilets

Shakopee Public Utilities states that the homeowner pays for all water used, including water lost through leaks. A constantly running toilet is one of the cheapest leaks to fix and one of the easiest to miss.

The same utility rates its water at 21 grains per gallon, which it describes as very hard. Mineral deposits can foul fill valve seats and flapper edges, which is one reason these parts wear out sooner in hard water areas.

Whether you replace the valve or call for help, shutting off the stop under the tank is the fastest way to stop an active leak.

When to stop and call a plumber

Signs this is more than a fill valve

  • The shutoff stop will not turn or leaks when you touch it.
  • The tank or bowl is cracked or water appears at the base of the toilet.
  • The toilet rocks, which can mean a failed wax ring or loose flange.
  • Water keeps running after a new valve and flapper are installed.
  • The toilet backs up or gurgles along with other fixtures.

Our toilet repair service covers wax rings, flanges and cracked fixtures. If water is spilling or the stop will not close, call the 24/7 line. Homes in Chanhassen and Chaska are on hard city water too, and the same fix applies.

Plumbing code note

Fill valves in the United States are expected to include anti-siphon protection under plumbing codes such as the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), so choose a valve marked for that.
